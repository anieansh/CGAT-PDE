About the product

McKesson's Cell & Gene Therapy platform.

InspiroLink is McKesson's enterprise ordering platform for Cell & Gene Therapies — a class of treatments where a single order can exceed $4M and mistakes have life-critical consequences. The system connects hospitals, clinics, manufacturers, and patients through a configurable approval and ordering workflow.

The challenge

Five problems no one had solved yet.

Cell & Gene Therapy ordering sits at the intersection of high-stakes compliance, complex organizational hierarchies, and real-time supply chain pressure. Here's what made this genuinely hard.

01

No standardized ordering model — every organization had a different understanding of what an "order" even meant.

02

Multi-level approval chains varied per org, per therapy type, and per order value — and all of it had to be configurable without engineering involvement.

03

PHI & compliance requirements meant every data field needed sensitivity classification, audit trails, and role-based visibility.

04

Validation logic was expressed as raw regex in a database — admins needed to write and test these rules without knowing regex.

05

Section 508 compliance was required from day one for government-adjacent hospital systems — not a retrofit.

03
Workflow journey

From discovery to a system that holds.

Discovery

Stakeholder interviews & process mapping

Mapped existing ordering flows across 3 hospital systems to identify where manual steps created bottlenecks and compliance gaps.

Definition

Mental model synthesis

Synthesized 40+ hours of research into a configurable data model — separating what needed to be enforced by the system vs. what needed to be delegated to admins.

Design

Approval group architecture & regex builder

Designed the two highest-complexity modules: a visual approval group builder and a no-code regex rule editor — both had to serve non-technical admins.

Validation

Usability testing with admins

5 rounds of moderated testing with real clinical operations staff. Key finding: admins trusted the system more when they could see the effect of a rule before saving it.

Delivery

Design system & handoff

Built a component library covering 60+ patterns across 4 domains. Spec'd accessibility at the component level — not as a final audit.

04
Deep dives

The two hardest problems.

Of the 15+ features I designed, two required the most design invention. Here's the thinking behind each.

Deep dive 01

Approval Group Builder

Clinical operations teams needed to define multi-tiered approval chains — but the underlying data model was complex enough that it would take a developer weeks to configure in the database. I needed to make that configurable in a UI.

Approvers and approval groups aren't the same thing. Most admins conflated them. I separated the concepts visually — groups as containers, approvers as members — and used progressive disclosure to introduce complexity only when needed.
Tested both a nested tree UI and a flat sequential list. The flat list consistently tested better — admins understood "step 1 approves before step 2" more intuitively than parent-child nodes. Chose the flat list with explicit ordering controls.
A drag-and-drop approval step builder with inline member assignment, condition preview, and a simulation mode where admins could test a chain against a hypothetical order before activating it.
Deep dive 02

No-Code Regex Builder

The platform stored field-level validation rules as raw regex in the database. Admins needed to edit these — but regex is a programmer's tool. I had to make it usable by clinical operations staff who had never written a line of code.

Admins didn't think in patterns — they thought in examples. "It should look like this: NDC-12345-678-90." I used this insight to design an example-first interface where admins describe valid values, and the system infers the pattern.
Rather than expose regex syntax, I gave admins composable blocks: "must start with", "followed by N digits", "optional suffix", etc. Each block mapped to a regex fragment. The final pattern was always visible — but hidden behind a toggle for those who didn't need it.
Inline test input — type a sample value, see pass/fail immediately. This removed the anxiety of "did I configure it right?" and gave admins confidence to save rules without needing an engineer to verify.

05
Collaboration

Designing with — not for.

As the only designer on a cross-functional team, I embedded myself in every discipline.

Engineering
Paired on edge cases

Weekly syncs to walk through specs and catch implementation gaps before they became bugs. I wrote interaction notes directly in Jira tickets, not just Figma comments.

Product Management
Shaped the roadmap

Brought user research into sprint planning. Translated clinical ops feedback into prioritized design debt — not just feature requests.

Clinical Operations
Continuous co-design

5 rounds of usability testing with actual admins. Recruited participants, moderated sessions, and synthesized findings within the same sprint.

Compliance & Legal
508 from day one

Embedded Section 508 requirements into component specs early — not as a post-launch audit. Saved an estimated 3 months of remediation work.

Accessibility

Built in, not bolted on.

Healthcare systems serving government-adjacent clients require Section 508 conformance by law. I treated accessibility as an architectural concern from the first component.

1.4.3 / AA
Contrast Ratio

All text meets 4.5:1 minimum. Interactive surfaces meet 3:1.

2.4.7 / AA
Focus Visible

Custom focus ring system with 2px offset on all interactive elements.

1.3.1 / A
Info & Relationships

Semantic HTML enforced in the design system. No div-soup components.

2.1.1 / A
Keyboard Accessible

Full keyboard navigation including drag-and-drop fallbacks.

4.1.2 / A
Name, Role, Value

ARIA annotations on every custom component, reviewed with NVDA.

1.4.4 / AA
Resize Text

Meets 200% zoom. 400% reflow in scope for phase 2.

09
Reflection

What I'd improve today.

01
Approval flow simulation

A sandbox to dry-run a configured approval chain against sample orders before going live.

02
Analytics dashboards

Visibility into where orders stall so teams could improve their own configurations.

03
AI-assisted configuration

Suggesting approval groups and validation rules from an org's historical patterns.

04
Predictive validation

Catching likely-wrong data before submission, not just structurally-invalid data.

05
Design debt

A few early patterns hardened faster than they should have — I'd invest in the token layer sooner.

06
Lessons learned

In enterprise, the interface is the easy part. The real design work is in the model beneath it.

Design is about reducing complexity, not adding features.

Solving enterprise problems taught me that the most valuable design isn't the most visible. It's the structure underneath — the model, the constraints, the guardrails — that lets a complex product feel simple. That's the kind of work I want to keep doing.
