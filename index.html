About the product

McKesson's Cell & Gene Therapy platform.

InspiroLink is McKesson's enterprise ordering platform for Cell & Gene Therapies — a class of treatments where a single order can exceed $4M and mistakes have life-critical consequences.

The challenge

Five problems no one had solved yet.

Cell & Gene Therapy ordering sits at the intersection of high-stakes compliance, complex organizational hierarchies, and real-time supply chain pressure.

01

No standardized ordering model — every organization had a different understanding of what an "order" even meant.

02

Multi-level approval chains varied per org, per therapy type, and per order value — all configurable without engineering involvement.

03

PHI & compliance requirements meant every data field needed sensitivity classification, audit trails, and role-based visibility.

04

Validation logic was expressed as raw regex — admins needed to write and test rules without knowing regex.

05

Section 508 compliance was required from day one — not a retrofit.

03
Workflow journey

From discovery to a system that holds.

Discovery

Stakeholder interviews & process mapping

Mapped existing ordering flows across 3 hospital systems to identify bottlenecks and compliance gaps.

Definition

Mental model synthesis

Synthesized 40+ hours of research into a configurable data model — separating what the system enforces vs. what admins control.

Design

Approval group architecture & regex builder

Designed the two highest-complexity modules — both had to serve non-technical admins.

Validation

Usability testing with admins

5 rounds of moderated testing. Key finding: admins trusted the system more when they could see the effect of a rule before saving it.

Delivery

Design system & handoff

Built a component library covering 60+ patterns. Spec'd accessibility at the component level — not as a final audit.

04
Deep dives

The two hardest problems.

Of the 15+ features I designed, two required the most design invention.

Deep dive 01

Approval Group Builder

Clinical operations teams needed to define multi-tiered approval chains — configurable in a UI without developer involvement.

Approvers and approval groups aren't the same thing. Most admins conflated them. I separated the concepts visually — groups as containers, approvers as members — and used progressive disclosure to introduce complexity only when needed.
Tested both a nested tree UI and a flat sequential list. The flat list consistently tested better — admins understood "step 1 approves before step 2" more intuitively than parent-child nodes.
A drag-and-drop approval step builder with inline member assignment, condition preview, and a simulation mode to test a chain against a hypothetical order before activating it.
Deep dive 02

No-Code Regex Builder

Validation rules lived as raw regex in the database. Admins needed to edit them — without knowing regex.

Admins didn't think in patterns — they thought in examples. "It should look like: NDC-12345-678-90." I designed an example-first interface where admins describe valid values and the system infers the pattern.
Composable blocks: "must start with", "followed by N digits", "optional suffix". Each mapped to a regex fragment. The final pattern was always visible — hidden behind a toggle for those who didn't need it.
Inline test input — type a sample value, see pass/fail immediately. Removed the anxiety of "did I configure it right?" and gave admins confidence to save rules without engineering verification.
05
Collaboration

Designing with — not for.

As the only designer on a cross-functional team, I embedded myself in every discipline.

Engineering
Paired on edge cases

Weekly syncs to walk through specs and catch implementation gaps before they became bugs. Interaction notes written directly in Jira tickets.

Product Management
Shaped the roadmap

Brought user research into sprint planning. Translated clinical ops feedback into prioritized design debt — not just feature requests.

Clinical Operations
Continuous co-design

5 rounds of usability testing with actual admins. Recruited participants, moderated sessions, and synthesized findings within the same sprint.

Compliance & Legal
508 from day one

Embedded Section 508 requirements into component specs early — not as a post-launch audit. Saved an estimated 3 months of remediation work.

Impact

Meaningful outcomes — not invented metrics.

I'd rather be honest about impact than fabricate numbers. Here's what the work genuinely enabled.

⚙️
Configurable workflows across organizations

New orgs onboarded through configuration instead of custom engineering work.

⏱️
Reduced manual configuration effort

Presets and sensible defaults cut repetitive setup for admins and teams.

🔀
Simplified complex approval processes

A single system absorbed hierarchies that previously lived in code and spreadsheets.

👥
Improved engineering collaboration

Shared patterns and clear rationale reduced back-and-forth during build.

🧩
Created reusable interaction patterns

Forms, validation, and status patterns became building blocks for future features.

📈
Supported future scalability

The platform could grow to new therapies and org types without redesign.

09
Reflection

What I'd improve today.

01
Approval flow simulation

A sandbox to dry-run a configured approval chain against sample orders before going live.

02
Analytics dashboards

Visibility into where orders stall so teams could improve their own configurations.

03
AI-assisted configuration

Suggesting approval groups and validation rules from an org's historical patterns.

04
Predictive validation

Catching likely-wrong data before submission, not just structurally-invalid data.

05
Design debt

A few early patterns hardened faster than they should have — I'd invest in the token layer sooner.

06
Lessons learned

In enterprise, the interface is the easy part. The real design work is in the model beneath it.

Design is about reducing complexity, not adding features.

Solving enterprise problems taught me that the most valuable design isn't the most visible. It's the structure underneath — the model, the constraints, the guardrails — that lets a complex product feel simple. That's the kind of work I want to keep doing.
